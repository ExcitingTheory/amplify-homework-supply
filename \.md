Is it weird to have a picture on the right side if a website card and controls on the left
No, it is not inherently weird, but it goes against standard Western web patterns and shifts the cognitive priorities for your users. Because users in left-to-right (LTR) cultures read from left to right, placing interactive controls on the left means they will see and process the action choices before they look at the illustrative image.

Whether this layout works well depends entirely on the context and purpose of your card.
When Controls on the Left / Image on the Right Works
Action-First Tasks: If the card is part of a highly repetitive utility interface (like a data dashboard, a settings panel, or a file manager), users care more about the controls (checkboxes, toggle switches, or dropdowns) than the thumbnail image. Putting the controls on the left allows for rapid scanning and clicking.
Form-Heavy Layouts: If the card functions primarily as an input step in a wizard or questionnaire, keeping inputs aligned to the left margin creates a strong, predictable vertical visual axis for form completion.
The Trade-offs and Risks
Delayed Context: In e-commerce or content-heavy sites, the image usually provides the context for the action. If a user sees a "Buy Now" or "Select" button before they even see what the product looks like on the right, it can feel disorienting.

What if we just had these cards in a grid or carousel for all widthz
Switching from a vertical list layout to a Grid or a Carousel across all screen widths will fundamentally change how students interact with the assignments.
Because your cards represent required sequential coursework with varying lengths of text, a Grid is almost always superior to a Carousel.
Here is a direct evaluation of the two options to help you choose the best fit for your learning platform.
📊 Grid Layout vs. Carousel Layout
Feature	📐 Option A: The Responsive Grid	🎠 Option B: The Horizontal Carousel
UX Context	Excellent for task management. Shows all assignments simultaneously so students can gauge their total workload at a glance.	Excellent for casual browsing. Best suited for discovering elective courses or optional extra-credit content.
Completion Flow	Encourages completing tasks in chronological order as students scroll down naturally.	Hides future tasks behind click/swipe barriers, making it easy for students to accidentally skip assignments.
Mobile Behavior	Collapses gracefully into a single-column scrolling feed.	Keeps horizontal sliding, but cards become highly cramped or require micro-swiping.
Text Handling	Handles variable text heights easily by defining equal-height grid lines.	High risk of layout breaking. Cards with long text push the carousel's height boundaries unevenly. 
🎨 How to Build Them Right
If you decide to move forward with either layout, your original "image-on-the-right" design needs to shift. For both layouts, the image must move to the top of the card to serve as a uniform visual anchor.
1. Designing the Responsive Grid
To prevent cards from stretching or unevenly aligning when course descriptions vary in length, use a modern CSS layout rule:
Desktop: Apply grid-template-columns: repeat(auto-fill, minmax(300px, 1fr)) paired with grid-auto-rows: 1fr. This guarantees every single card in a row stays perfectly equal in height, regardless of text length.
Mobile: Let the cards naturally stack into a single column.

🚨 Why Moving it to the Right Breaks Grids and Carousels
The "Squish" Effect: Grid columns and carousels naturally limit a card's horizontal width (usually to around 280px–350px). If you split that tight space horizontally into text on the left and an image on the right, your text container becomes incredibly narrow. Long Japanese lesson titles will break into 3 or 4 ugly, single-word lines.
Visual Fragmentation: In a grid row of 3 or 4 cards, your eyes want to anchor on a single, predictable line. If images are stuck on the right of every small box, the layout feels jagged because the text width fluctuates on every single card depending on the aspect ratio of the thumbnail.
💡 The Verdict & Best Approach
If you keep a single vertical LIST layout (your original screenshot): Image on the right works perfectly fine because the card stretches full-width, giving the text plenty of breathing room to live on the left.
If you switch to a multi-column GRID or a CAROUSEL: The image must go on top (full card width). This stacks the elements cleanly, prevents text cramping, keeps your action buttons consistently aligned at the bottom, and maintains your platform's minimalist, accessible design principles.

This planning document aggregates the complete strategic design principles, architectural critiques, and reusable Material UI (MUI v5) technical layouts developed across our design session. It serves as a single source of truth for engineering the student and teacher interfaces.
📐 Section 1: Core Design Critique & Core UX Strategy
1.1 Structural Layout & Information Hierarchy
The Problem: The initial dashboard suffered from extreme layout duplication and information noise. Content tracking details, execution variables, and lesson details shared identical text scales. In the teacher view, individual student list tiles took up massive vertical real estate, forcing extensive page scrolling.
The Strategy: Break user layouts into clean, visually separated functional zones. Isolate high-impact interaction layers from baseline descriptive text, ensuring information layouts remain highly predictable and digestible.
1.2 Multi-Viewport Layout Systems (Desktop vs. Mobile)
Desktop (Horizontal Feed Layout): Standardize Left-to-Right reading dynamics as the structural baseline. Position illustrative graphic elements or course thumbnails on the left, typography and content columns in the center, and primary calls-to-action (CTAs) along the far-right margin. This aligns directly with natural eye tracking.
Grid & Carousel Structural Constraints: Never split narrow grid blocks or carousel frames horizontally (Image Right / Text Left). Doing so compresses text areas into unreadable strips, forcing awkward, broken word wrapping.
Mobile Viewport Folding: On compact screen shapes, dynamically drop horizontal layers into vertical structures. Force the thumbnail container to sit at full width on top to function as a visual header, positioning multi-line details underneath and buttons expanding to full width along the lower layout margin for thumb accessibility.
1.3 High-Impact Functional Polish
Eliminate Raw String Key Leakage: Ensure internal localization paths (pages.sectionDetail.*, components.assignmentCard.*) are fully resolved into text layers using active localized context bundles before rendering in the DOM tree.
Establish Clear Call-to-Action (CTA) Weights: Avoid identical buttons side-by-side. Secondary utility paths must use lightweight text configurations, while main goal tasks highlight themselves via high-contrast solid filled backgrounds.
Anchor Alerts Inline with Content Contexts: Move global error warnings down out of top screen margins directly into respective modular section boundaries. This immediately alerts teachers to specific items requiring swift intervention without forcing them to trace back and forth across the screen.
